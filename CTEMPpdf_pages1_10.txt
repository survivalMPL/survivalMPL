This paper considers simultaneous estimation of the regression coefficients and baseline
hazard in proportional hazard models using the maximum penalized likelihood (MPL)
method where a penalty function is used to smooth the baseline hazard estimate. Although
MPL methods exist to fit proportional hazard models, they suffer from the following
deficiencies: (i) the positivity constraint on the baseline hazard estimate is either avoided or
poorly treated leading to efficiency loss, (ii) the asymptotic properties of the MPL estimator
are lacking, and (iii) simulation studies comparing the performance of MPL to that of the
partial likelihood have not been conducted. In this paper we propose a new approach and
aim to address these issues. We first model baseline hazard using basis functions, then
estimate this approximate baseline hazard and the regression coefficients simultaneously.
The penalty function included in the likelihood is quite general but typically assumes prior
knowledge about the smoothness of the baseline hazard. A new iterative optimization
algorithm, which combines Newton’s method and a multiplicative iterative algorithm,
is developed and its convergence properties studied. We show that if the smoothing
parameter tends to zero sufficiently fast, the new estimator is consistent, asymptotically
normal and retains full efficiency under independent censoring. A simulation study reveals
that this method can be more efficient than the partial likelihood method, particularly for
small to moderate samples. In addition, our simulation shows that the new estimator is
substantially less biased under informative censoring.

1. Introduction
The proportional hazard (or Cox’s regression) model (e.g. Cox (1972) and Kalbfleisch and Prentice (2002, Chapter 4)) is
one of the most widely used model for analyzing time to event (survival time) data. Regression coefficients of proportional
hazard models, however, are usually estimated by maximizing the Cox’s partial likelihood (PL) function (Cox, 1975), where
the baseline hazard is not required when estimating the regression coefficients. The popularity of PL is mainly due to: (i) its
ability to avoid estimating the baseline hazard function when the main focus is on estimating the regression coefficients, and
(ii) asymptotic properties of the maximum partial likelihood estimate, such as in Tsiatis (1981) and Andersen and Gill (1982).
When the baseline hazard is also of interest it can be estimated from the Breslow estimate (Breslow, 1972) or smoothed
versions of it based on a kernel (Gray, 1990) or penalized likelihood smoothing (Anderson and Senthilselvan, 1980). The
PL approach, however, is not always easy to extend to more general models or complex censoring schemes. For instance,

In this paper we adopt the MPL method to fit the Cox model. Earlier relevant references include Gray (1994), Joly
et al. (1998) and Cai and Betensky (2003) where splines were used to approximate the baseline hazard for left or right
censoring. Gray (1994) focused on testing hypotheses on covariate effects in the Cox model while others considered the
estimation problem. Different penalty functions, such as roughness penalties, were considered.
However, existing methods suffer from some of the following deficiencies. Firstly, the way the positivity constraint on the
baseline hazard is dealt with is suboptimal. It is either ignored (Gray, 1994) or ensured through ‘‘ad-hoc’’ techniques like the
use of squares of splines coefficients (Joly et al., 1998), which potentially creates convergence issues (especially when some
coefficients are zero). Others like Cai and Betensky (2003) prefer to model the log-baseline hazard as a linear spline, leading
to difficulties in obtaining the cumulative baseline hazard in closed form, and also linear spline is inflexible. Secondly, the
asymptotic properties are rarely discussed or rigorously presented. Thirdly, simulation studies comparing their performance
to PL’s are lacking, casting doubt on the real advantages of these approaches. This paper attempts to address these issues for
a new MPL proposal. Specifically the non-negativity constraint is ensured by directly restricting the coefficients of the basis
functions, and we develop a new algorithm computing simultaneously the MPL estimates of the regression coefficients and
baseline hazard. The asymptotic properties of the MPL estimator are derived when (a) the smoothing parameter converges to
zero sufficiently fast, or (b) when it does not converge to zero. Its performance with respect to PL is assessed by simulations.
We develop our method in the context of proportional hazard model and right censoring in this paper. But it is, in
principle, general enough to accommodate a large class of censoring schemes including interval censoring. Extensions
to different survival models such as additive or accelerate failure time models are clearly possible but will be reported
elsewhere.
This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we review the standard formulation of the Cox model and introduce
the MPL approach. In Section 3 we propose a new alternating algorithm for computing the MPL estimates of the regression
coefficients and baseline hazard and prove its convergence. The asymptotic properties of the MLP estimates are developed in
Section 4, where consistency and asymptotic normality of the MPL estimator are obtained when the smoothing parameter
is of order o(n−1/2). In Section 5 a simulation study comparing the performance of PL and MPL under various levels of
censoring is conducted. Under independent censoring MPL provides more efficient estimates than PL for small to moderate
sample sizes, with the gain vanishing for large samples. Moreover, we find from the simulation that the MPL estimates are
consistently less biased than the PL’s under dependent censoring, particularly for large censoring proportions and sample
sizes. In some cases the gain in efficiency is substantial. The new approach is also illustrated using a critical care dataset in
Section 5. Finally, concluding remarks are given in Section 6.
2. Fitting proportional hazard model by maximum penalized likelihood
2.1. Model and notation
For simplicity, we assume continuous survival times throughout this paper. We denote the independent survival times
by t1, . . . , tn (obtained from n individuals), where some of these observations are right censored. To simplify notations we

3. Algorithm
3.1. Principle
We propose an iterative algorithm to solve the optimization problem (7) under the constraint θ ≥0. In this algorithm,
the two components β and θ are updated alternately in each iteration. Specifically, β is updated using the Newton–Raphson
algorithm while θ is renewed with the multiplicative iterative (MI) algorithm of Ma (2010). Note that more traditional
constrained optimization methods, such as those given in Luenberger (1984, Chapter 11), are not used for θ mainly because
they are inefficient when the second derivative of Φ with respect to θ is poorly scaled (i.e. has a large condition number),
which is common for the proportional hazard model. The MI algorithm only demands the first derivative, making it very
easy to derive and implement.
The MI algorithm (Ma, 2010) was originally developed for penalized likelihood tomographic image reconstructions. These
reconstruction problems are equivalent to constrained generalized linear models (GLMs) with huge dimensions (at least
1282 non-negative regression parameters) and where the number of observations is usually smaller than the number of
regression parameters. MI is an efficient non-negatively constrained algorithm which requires only the first derivative of
the objective function. Its application can be extended to general constrained parameters estimation problems including
hazard estimation.

3.3. Convergence properties
Due to the line search, the modified Newton algorithm in Step 1 guarantees the objective function Φ(β, θ(k)) increases
when β moves from β(k) to β(k+1). Similarly, the line search procedure in Step 2 ensures that Φ(β(k+1), θ) increases
when θ changes from θ(k) to θ(k+1). Thus after updating both parameter β and θ at iteration k + 1, Φ(β(k+1), θ(k+1)) ≥
Φ(β(k+1), θ(k)) ≥Φ(β(k), θ(k)), where equalities hold only when iterations have converged. This property, together with
the facts that β and θ updates are bounded and iteration mappings are continuous, leads to the convergence result of the
Newton-MI algorithm. Results are stated in Theorem 1 and its proof is given in Appendix A.
Theorem 1. Consider the Newton-MI algorithm defined in Section 2. Assume β ∈Rp, the p dimensional real space, and assume
elements of θ are all non-negative, i.e. θ ≥0. Let M1 and M2 be respectively the iteration mappings for the modified Newton and
the MI components of the Newton-MI algorithm, so that β(k+1) = M1(β(k); θ(k)) and θ(k+1) = M2(θ(k); β(k+1)). Assume matrix
A defined in Eq. (13) satisfies that A1/2X has full column rank for β ∈Rp and θ ≥0. With starting values θ(0) > 0 and β(0) ∈Rp,
the Newton-MI algorithm produces a sequence {β(k), θ(k)} converging to a solution satisfying the KKT conditions (11) and (12).

5. Results
This section reports the results from a simulation study and from a real data application. In the simulation study our aims
are threefold: (i) demonstrate that the proposed method of MPL estimation of β and θ works well, (ii) assess the efficiency
of the MPL estimates and compare them to their PL counterpart under independent and dependent censoring schemes, and
(iii) evaluate the asymptotic variance of the MPL estimate of β. All the results in this section are obtained using MATLAB.
Although only one simulation is reported in this section, other simulations had also been conducted and similar results were
observed.
The basis functions used in this section are indicator functions, leading to a piecewise constant (or discretization)
approximation to h0(t). To simplify notations we denote the discretized h0(t) by vector(θ1, . . . , θm)T , where m is the number
of bins for the discretization. In this simulation we choose the equal bin event count, where the common event count of each
bin is denoted by n0. This choice is more efficient than equal bin width as it better manipulates the information contained
in the data. In the simulation scenarios below we report n0 in each example.
Corresponding to discretization, any derivative operation in a penalty function should be replaced by difference. The
square of the first order difference penalty, namely

5.1. A simulation with independent censoring
In this example, we simulate survival times yi from the Weibull distribution with hazard
hi(t) = 3t2 exp{xi1 + 0.1xi2 −0.5xi3}.
(31)
For each yi a censoring time ci is generated independently of yi from the exponential distribution Exp(µc). The mean µc is
calibrated to achieve, on average, a pre-specified censoring level πc. The observed data are {(ti, ξi), i = 1, . . . , n}, where ti =
min{yi, ci} andξi = I(yi < ci), where I is the indicator function. Note that ci in this example are generated independently of ti.
Model (31) is a proportional hazard model with baseline hazard h0(t) = 3t2 and regression coefficients β1 = 1, β2 = 0.1
and β3 = −0.5. Values for covariates x1, x2 and x3 are generated from uniform distributions: xi1 ∼unif(0, 1), xi2 ∼
unif(0, 5) and xi3 ∼unif(0, 7).
To have a clear picture on how each estimation method is affected by the sample size n and censoring proportion, we
choose n =100, 500, and 5000 corresponding respectively to small, intermediate and large sample sizes, with approximate
censoring proportions πc of 20%, 50% and 80% for each value of n. Regression coefficients β1, β2 and β3 are estimated by
Cox’s PL and our MPL.
As mentioned above, the ‘‘equal bin event count’’ strategy is used to define the indicator functions. Preliminary tests
showed that in general the β estimates are not very sensitive to the choice of the common bin event count n0 as long as it is
not too large and the smoothing value is selected appropriately. In practice, we set n0 = 1 for n = 100, n0 = 3 for n = 500
and n0 = 10 for n = 5000, decreasing the ratio n0/n as more data are available. Convergence of the Newton-MI algorithm
is achieved if the difference between updates of β and θ from two consecutive iterations is less than 10−5. In the tables
below we present λ = γ /(1 −γ ), so that the tuning parameter γ satisfies γ ∈[0, 1). The γ values given in Table 1 are
chosen experimentally and are not particularly fine-tuned. They are nevertheless small enough to guarantee consistency for
the regression parameter (see Theorem 2). Note that small samples generally need lighter smoothing while large samples
require heavier smoothing; this is because fewer bins are used for small samples than for large samples.
For each combination of n and πc, 500 repeated samples are generated and thus 500 estimates of β and θ are obtained.
Performance of different β estimates are assessed by examining their biases and standard deviations (stds) or mean squared
errors (MSEs), all obtained from the Monte Carlo simulation.

5.2. Simulation results with informative censoring
We also study the performance of PL and MPL under dependent censoring although none of them is particularly built to
deal with this situation. Here the objective is to see how sensitive the PL and MPL estimates are when informative censoring
is present but ignored (as sometimes dependent censoring cannot be easily identified).
The model used in the previous section to generate survival data is also used here. Again random survival times y1, . . . , yn
are generated from the Weibull distribution with the same hazard function as (31). The censoring status of each yi is however
generated differently. First for a given censoring proportion πc we decide whether an observation yi is to be censored. If so,
the corresponding censoring time ti is generated from the unif(0, yi) distribution and the corresponding censoring indicator
ξi is set to 0; otherwise, ti = yi and ξi is set to 1. Clearly, censoring times generated in this way are dependent on the
true survival times, leading to informative censoring. For consistency, the sample sizes, censoring proportions, and penalty
function remain unchanged from the previous example. The ‘‘equal bin event count’’ option for MPL, where n0 = 1 for
n = 100, n0 = 3 for n = 500 and n0 = 20 for n = 5000, is used. The smoothing values are selected again experimentally;
they are γ = 0.8 for n = 100 and γ = 0.9 for both n = 500 and n = 5000. In general larger smoothing parameters than
for the independent censoring case need to be used. This is required to give more accurate θ estimates probably leading to
less biased β estimates.
